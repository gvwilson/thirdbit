Closing Time


Communicating During a Deliberate Shutdown

What Clinicians Get Wrong

  • Clinicians systematically fail patients by focusing on what medicine can do rather than asking what the patient actually wants [Gawande2014]
  • Project closure conversations fail the same way
  • Discussions should:
    • Establish a shared understanding of the situation
    • Make each party's values and priorities explicit
    • Negotiate a plan that meets those goals, not just what's technically easiest
  • Focus on the least bad outcome for each stakeholder and what trade-offs they'll accept

Honest Enough

  • Framing matters: "hibernation" provokes less hostility than "termination"
    • If it is the truth and credible
  • "Tell people what you know, as soon as you know it" — easy to say, hard to do
    • Information gaps cause more anxiety than difficult truths
    • But you often don't know what's happening
    • Constantly-changing pronouncements raise the temperature too
  • Information vacuums do more damage than honest announcements [Wruck1990]

Mechanics

  • Announce that you are stopping, not that the project is shutting down
    • Invite known successors to take over (no supply chain attack risk)
  • Give real deadlines: 2–6 months between announcement and shutdown
  • Use all announcement channels, not just the README
  • Update the project home page:
    • Key achievements, current state, archive locations, alternatives for users
  • Don't bother with: tutorials, exhaustive docs, new tests, bug triage

The Human Side

  • Have someone outside the project run structured exit interviews
    • Focus on what should survive, not what went wrong
  • Hold a wake: a combination retrospective and memorial
    • What went well, what didn't, and what stories people will remember

Dignity Therapy

[Chochinov2005, Chochinov2012] advocate dignity therapy as a brief, structured intervention for people near the end of life. The central technique is a guided life-review interview: what has mattered most, what they are proud of, and what they want future generations to know. Edited into a permanent legacy document that extends influence beyond death.

A similar structured exit interview at the end of a project is more than a conventional "lessons learned" report — it is personal, narrative, and explicitly oriented toward what should survive.

How Leaders Leave

  • [Sonnenfeld1988] identified four departure archetypes:
    • Monarchs: refuse to leave, undermine successors
    • Generals: depart reluctantly, maintain ties, attempt to return
    • Ambassadors: leave willingly, remain available, most satisfied
    • Governors: leave cleanly, disengage entirely
  • Recognizing your pattern in advance shapes the handover conversations

Write a Shutdown Notice

  1. Draft a short announcement (3-5 bullet points) for your own project.
  2. Swap and identify one thing the other notice does well and one thing it could improve.

Stakeholder Message Matrix

Draft one-sentence messages for three audiences:

  1. An email to a researcher who uses the data pipeline daily in their own analysis scripts.
  2. A note on an outstanding pull request for a contributor who submitted three other pull requests in the past year.
  3. A written notice to the project's institutional funder.

Leak Response

  1. Draft the message you send to your team in the first 30 minutes. What do you tell them, and what do you ask them not to do?
  2. Draft the public post you publish within the next 2 hours. How does the premature leak change the tone, timing, and content of what you had planned to say?
  3. In groups, discuss which stakeholder relationships are most at risk from the leak. What can you realistically salvage, and what is likely already damaged beyond repair?
