Why This Matters

  • For every beginning there must be an ending
  • But we don't like to talk about that
    • Thousands of books about how to start a business
    • Only a handful about how to pass one on or wind one down
    • Most of those are really about how to sell out
  • Preparing for the end can make it less painful
    • And less likely: thinking about endings helps us prevent some of them
    • It also makes what happens before the end more enjoyable

Two Kinds of Ending

  • Deliberate closure
    • You made the decision or have enough notice to plan
    • You can learn as you go
  • Abrupt closure
    • Short notice, usually not your decision
    • You get one shot and can't fix mistakes
  • We want the former but often face the latter

Best Practices vs. Reality

  • [Wilson2014] outlined how research software engineers should organize projects
  • [Wilson2017] was in part a rebuttal
    • Most people don't have time to do everything the "right" way
    • Comparing their work to "best practices" is like comparing a surgeon at leisure in a well-equipped hospital to a paramedic at a crash site
  • This workshop addresses both cases
    • Vaida has time to plan and correct mistakes
    • Liam only has time to do a few things right the first time

Abstinence Solutions

An abstinence solution is one that requires people to do things we know they actually won't (see also "bullshit" [Frankfurt2005]). In particular, "document everything" doesn't work. People will always short-change writing descriptions of work in favor of actually doing work, and fail to write down the parts of their work that they think are obvious. This isn't laziness: most of what we can write today doesn't actually address tomorrow's actual needs.

The Survival Arc

  • [Ripley2008] identified a three-stage survival arc:
    • Denial: refusal to believe the evidence
    • Deliberation: paralysed uncertainty about what to do
    • Decision: action, which may or may not be correct
  • Denial usually consumes far more time than people expect
    • Thinking through the scenario beforehand dramatically shortens it
  • Rehearsal converts a novel emergency into a recognized pattern
  • Business owners satisfied with their exits started planning years in advance [Burlingham2014]

What We Can Hope For

  • The sunk cost fallacy keeps projects alive past their useful life
  • [Weitzel1989] describes five stages of organisational decline:
    • Blindness → Inaction → Faulty action → Crisis → Dissolution
  • The moment shutdown becomes undeniable is rarely the moment it became inevitable [Whetten1980]
  • Recognizing which stage a project is in determines which interventions are still available

Threat Models

  • Use threat modeling from computer security to think about external factors
  • Individual threats: affect one or a few team members
    • Career/life changes, visa revocations
  • Leadership threats: targeted at the project's leader
    • E.g., media targeting for politically unpopular findings
  • Community threats: affect large groups
    • Department shutdowns, Climategate
  • Global threats: affect everyone
    • Current attacks on science in the United States

Stay Within the Law

  • Ensuring work remains usable is not worth legal risk
    • Institutions, journals, and funders have policies for licensing and sharing [Katz2018]
    • Know the rules (which isn't the same as "follow them" [OSS1944])
  • If you don't know if there's a policy, ask someone
    • Look for an Open Source Program Office or IP office
  • Use email rather than chat or video calls
    • You need a record of everything said
    • People may change their minds about what they said, meant, or promised

Rules as Written vs. Rules as Enforced

There is often a difference in large bureaucratic organizations between the rules as written and the rules as enforced. If you are leaving on short notice or under difficult circumstances, you may want to be selective about which forms you fill in proactively. Members of marginalized groups have had more practice with these tactics than members of privileged groups [Scott1987].

Quick Check

  1. Are you handing over or shutting down?
    • Think of shutdown as "succession to zero"
  2. Do you have days or months to prepare?
    • The former is usually involuntary; the latter allows for structural changes
  3. Can you act unilaterally or do other people have a veto?
    • Are those "other people" your peers or your superiors?
  4. Can you talk to someone who has been through this before?
