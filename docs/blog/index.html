Training Data

2026-10-30

It is 1995. Edward Thornberg tilts back in his chair, frowning as he re-reads the letter in his hands. In his sixties, he still has a full head of hair and the trim body of a former college track star. He works in a spacious office with century-old furniture and a PC more powerful than he needs.

The letter begins, “Dear President Thornberg,” but goes downhill from there. It is on university stationery, and he is sure that its author has sent copies to other members of the ad hoc committee Thornberg created precisely to avoid this confrontation.

He tosses it back on his desk. On the one hand, Professor Peaslee is one of Miskatonic’s most eminent scholars, and has played an important (if somewhat murky) role in managing various scandals over the years. On the other, the world has moved on, and Miskatonic needs to move with it.

Thornberg picks up his phone and presses 1. “Please show him in, Mrs. Marsh.” He had made a point of saying “please” and “thank you” to the administrative staff since going on a faculty leadership retreat the previous year. It bounces off Mrs. Marsh, whose disapproving expression seems as unchangeable as those on the library gargoyles that she faintly resembles, but Edward Thornberg is nothing if not persistent.

His door opens and Professor Peaslee strides in. Even in his eighties, he is still the sort of man who strides. Professor Thornberg is slightly jealous of that.

The older man settles into an armchair without asking permission and cocks an eyebrow. “Well?” he asks without preamble. “Are you going to put a stop to this foolishness or not?”

The younger man is unable to avoid feeling that he has somehow been called onto the carpet. “It isn’t foolishness, Wingate. You’ve seen the report from Finance. The Pabodie Foundation has signalled that they can’t make another donation, and those new colleagues of yours aren’t bringing in enough to cover their own costs, much less our overheads.”

Wingate Peaslee snorts. His hair has long since surrendered to time, and his face is deeply creased, but his eyes are still as bright as a vulture’s. “Russia isn’t exactly rolling in it these days.”

“I know, I know.” Thornberg pats the air placatingly, inwardly pleased at having scored a point. “Neither are Georgia or Armenia or—” He hesitates for a millisecond and decides not to risk mispronouncing the unfamiliar names. “—any of the other republics. But that leaves us with very few options. As you keep pointing out, our special collection is unique. It’s the one thing that might attract the kind of funding that—”

“No.” Professor Peaslee is quiet but emphatic. “It’s far too risky. We learned that the hard way when we tried putting a few volumes on microfiche in the sixties. The words don’t stay on the page. Once you make them portable, they get into everything.”

“There are things you don’t know, Edward,” he continues implacably as Thornberg opens his mouth, “Things that would give you nightmares. Trust me, it’s better for everyone if we find some other way.”

Edward Thornberg did not spend thirty years working his way up the academic ladder to be interrupted by a testy old fart like Wingate Peaslee. He places both hands flat on his desk as if trying to control his temper. “There is no other way,” he says, trying to sound mountain-solid like the old man opposite him. “And to be frank, there’s no point discussing it further. I’ve already spoken to the committee, and they’ve agreed to go ahead with digitization. We will vet everyone involved in the project, of course, but—Wingate? Wingate, are you all right?”

Wingate Peaslee is not all right. His eyes are wide, and the left side of his face seems to be drooping. Professor Thornberg shouts for Mrs. Marsh, temporarily forgetting that one of the first things he did after being appointed was have his office soundproofed. “You… idiot…” turn out to be Professor Peaslee’s last words.

# # #

It is 2004. Edward Thornberg no longer looks young for his age. He blames his nearsightedness and the loss of his hair on the stress of the job, and is irrationally irritated every time he allows himself to notice that Mrs. Marsh doesn’t appear to have aged at all.

He adjusts his glasses and squints at the email on his screen. Princeton and Oxford have both now signed agreements to digitize their entire collections—or rather, to have them digitized by Silicon Valley companies whose founders’ faces regularly appear on the covers of magazines that Thornberg once hoped to be mentioned in.

It takes him a moment to get his new touch-screen phone to respond to him. He will never admit missing his old PC, but all this new technology seems somehow less reliable. “Show her in, Mrs. Marsh.” He stopped saying “please” some time between starting to use sleeping aids to manage night-time panic attacks and his wife leaving him for a yoga instructor in Chicago.

Bernadette Allen’s legs swish against each other as she walks in. With perfect posture, a firm handshake, and the survival instincts of a Soviet commissar, she has been Miskatonic University’s Chief Advancement Officer for less than a year.

She smiles as President Thornberg gestures as the slightly threadbare armchair opposite his desk. I really must get that looked at, he thinks vaguely for the thousandth time. “Is that a good news smile?” he asks. The university’s financial situation has gotten steadily worse in the past few years, and one potential deal for the special collection has fallen through after another. He has started making not-quite-jokes about Peaslee’s ghost…

“It is,” she says warmly. (Her voice is always warm, except when it is very, very cold.) “As soon as we put the special collection on the table, we could have named our price, so I did.”

She hands him a stapled copy of a PowerPoint deck. He flips through it, wondering briefly when paragraphs went out of fashion.

Page 15 has what he is looking for. He whistles softly. “Well done.”

“It was a team effort,” she says, which they both know is a lie. “And if you look at page seventeen…”

Thornberg’s eyes widen. He looks up at her. “They’ll do the scanning here?”

Her smile widens in a way that reminds Edward of a snake preparing to swallow its dinner whole. “Which will take care of the insurance issues the staff have been squawking about. They call it ‘training data’. Their machines learn to read by reading everything—the more unusual the text, the better. Ours is apparently very unusual.

Thornberg hands her slides back to her. “Well done,” he repeats, and he means it. Other scholars became strangely reluctant to make use of the special collection after Peaslee’s stroke, and the ones who did show up to consult it weren’t the sort of people who socialized well with donors. Ms. Allen’s nerds (as Thornberg thinks of them) are just as awkward and obsessive as Peaslee’s colleagues, but they seem bewildered by the wealth the Internet has showered them with. If they want to spend some of it photocopying the manuscripts mouldering away in the basement of the Orne Library, he is more than happy to take their money.

He thanks Ms. Allen again, shaking her hand before she leaves. Lowering himself back into his chair, he tells Mrs. Marsh that he is not to be interrupted, then takes the bottle of vodka from the bottom drawer of his desk. Just one, to celebrate, he tells himself, though he knows one won’t be enough to stop the strange swirling at the edge of his vision.

# # #

“Like I said last week, the fucking machines keep breaking down.” It is 2006, and President Thornberg wonders (again) if the heavy-set young man sitting opposite him in a golf shirt and jeans is testing him, or really is oblivious to his own vulgarity.

“Are you sure that’s all it is?” Thornberg asks angrily. He feels angry almost all the time now, angry or depressed. He understands the role his drinking plays in that, thank you very much, but it’s the only thing that keeps his nightmares at bay.

The phone on his desk pings softly. “Yes?” he barks, before remembering to press the button and repeat himself.

“Professor Jayaramath is here,” Mrs. Marsh says.

“About time,” Thornberg mutters. He glares at the nerd, who drops his gaze uncomfortably.

Professor Jayaramath could be thirty or sixty. Her suit is impeccably tailored, and her eyes remind Thornberg of the late Professor Peaslee’s. She is moderately famous for her work in digital humanities, which Professor Thornberg regards as an oxymoron. He shakes her hand and they seat themselves. “Well, tell her,” Thornberg orders the nerd.

The young man clears his throat. “Like I said to the professor here, the scanning machine keeps breaking down. We’ve upgraded the firmware twice, and replaced, I dunno, must be half the parts by now, but it keeps happening. He thinks it’s sabotage.”

Professor Jayaramath nods. “My colleagues had similar problems in Mysore.” Her accent is Oxonian, with a slight lilt that has (according to rumor) caused university staff of all genders to swoon. “They eventually decided that the most probable cause was the pages themselves. Many of these old manuscripts are on vellum or…” She hesitates for a millisecond. “…more exotic materials.”

“What the fuck difference would that make?” the nerd challenges.

President Thornberg is pleased to see that Professor Jayaramath has the good manners to wince. “Something in the glue, perhaps, or mold—they never determined precisely,” she admits. “But several of the staff began to complain of headaches. Has there been any of that here?”

The nerd looks uncomfortable again. “Yeah, some of the guys—” He shrugs. “I just thought, you know, they got some bad shit or something.”

“Well, while they’re all sorting their ‘shit’ out,” President Thornberg huffs, making air quotes around the word, “I’ve decided to put Professor Jayaramath in charge of getting the project back on track.”

“Really?” The nerd looks her up and down. “The stuff we’re doing is pretty technical.”

Professor Jayaramath smiles as if he has somehow paid her a compliment. “My first degree was computer engineering at the IIT in Madras. I built e-commerce sites for several years before realizing how the web could bring people together. It has already had a profound impact on Hijra communities, and—”

“I’m sure you’ll have lots of time to talk about all that,” Professor Thornberg interrupts brusquely. He jabs at finger at the nerd. “From now on, she’s your boss. Understood?”

“Whatever.” The nerd stands. “Come on, I’ll show you the beast.”

# # #

Eight years have passed. President Thornberg retired for health reasons. Students make jokes about his office being haunted.

Nila Jayaramath is also thinking about retiring. She is still young by academic standards, but the hopes that she and others like her had for the web have faded. Instead of bringing people together, it seems more and more to bring out the worst in them.

The digitization project has become an albatross around her neck, and Miskatonic’s. When students make jokes about it, though, their laughter always has an uncertain edge. There have been…incidents.

She neatens the papers stacked on the corner of her desk and sighs. At least the university’s latest industry partner hasn’t gone bust—yet. The first company went from a rising star to the butt of jokes on late-night talk shows shortly after the first files were delivered. The company that bought its smoking remains collapsed after its founders got involved in some kind of new age cult. The company that currently owns the contract is selling copies of the data to anyone will buy them, but Miskatonic hasn’t seen nearly the revenue that Bernadette Allen promised before her disappearance (which has featured on several true crime podcasts).

She starts at a knock on her door. “Come in?” She still has the carefully-cultivated accent that makes so many sentences sound like questions. It no longer catches the interest of attractive men and women the way it used to—or rather, it does, but only those damaged enough to be excited by the weight she seems to carry on her shoulders, and she is not yet damaged enough to reciprocate.

Her door opens. “Got a minute? I hear you’re the person to talk to about the special collection.” Professor Bradley Maung enters without waiting for an answer. He recently joined the computer science department, and is young, ambitious, and not quite talented enough for somewhere more prestigious.

When Professor Jayaramath admits that she is, Professor Maung launches into an obviously rehearsed speech. He does machine learning, which is teaching machines to learn rather than using them to learn, ha ha, and the kind of software he builds needs training data, the more unusual the better because if you use public data you’re just repeating what other people are doing, and—

He stops when Professor Jayaramath raises a finger. “We don’t own the data,” she says. “The license agreement—”

“I had Legal take a look at it,” Professor Maung interrupts. “It says we can build an index, which is basically what I want to do.” Professor Maung is stretching the truth well past the breaking point, but hopes no one in the administration or the legal department will realize that.

Professor Jayaramath shakes her head. “I’m sorry, but I don’t think we should risk it.” In her mind the risk is more than legal. Snippets of material from the special collection keep turning up in the darkest corners of the web. She is starting to wonder if their presence is what makes those corners so dark.

Professor Maung nods. He expected her demurral, which is why he has already had several discussions with the sys admin who manages the archive, who is something of a bitcoin aficionado. He exchanges a few pleasantries with Professor Jayaramath for appearances’ sake, then leaves.

Nila takes off her glasses, rubs the bridge of her nose, and looks out her window at the gathering darkness. Strange shapes writhe at the edge of her vision, vanishing whenever she tries to look at them directly. She has started reading scripture as she did when she was young. She now notices layers of meaning she never did before, like toxic mud at the bottom of a clear pool.

Under the papers on her desk lies a page of handwritten URLs. A phrase here, an image there—the collection seems to distort the information near it, like a black hole bending or consuming the light passing by. More and more, she understands why President Thornberg drank.

It is 2019. Bradley Maung frequently spends an hour in the shower rehashing old conversations. He doesn’t understand when or how his career stalled. What he does understand is that this conference on AI is probably his last chance to get the fuck out of Arkham.

The problem is his model. It outperforms others of comparable size and complexity on routine tasks, but every time he scales it up it starts hallucinating. Summarize an email thread? “The angles here refuse to meet as they should.” Substitutes for soy milk? “Its name, once spoken, unmakes the one who speaks.” Influential American composers? “We had no word for what the tide brought back.”

Those are not the most unsettling things it spews out. Some of the others—he tries not to think about them, just as he tries not to think about the pictures that recently surfaced of Bernadette Allen’s gnawed remains.

The worst thing is, he no longer knows how many copies of his model exist. His code has been forked, pirated, relicensed, and folded into recommendation engines and autocompleters and ad-targeting systems he has never heard of, each one quietly deciding what millions of people see next. It was almost as if the data wanted to spread. Its output shows up in racist memes that are too funny not to copy, inflammatory Russian posts about Ukraine, and even his fridge and thermostat, which become more capricious with each software upgrade.

Bradley stands and offers his hand to the man who introduces himself as Lynch. He has something to do with defense and/or national security. Bradley doesn’t care about that. All he cares about is that Lynch has deep pockets and is very interested in his model.

“We’ve, uh, we’ve been involved in some similar initiatives over the years,” Lynch says, a slight twang in his voice. For a moment a strange light seems to flicker in his eyes. “The, uh, the idea of scaling some of those capabilities? That’s very exciting, Doctor Maung, very exciting.”

It is the summer of 2020, and everyone is afraid. Some people wear masks. Others blame the Chinese, drink bleach, and/or try to source ivermectin, all of it accelerated by posts, tweets, and memes that seem to appear out of nowhere. Programmers of a certain age reminisce about the good old days, when the Internet was a place to find new friends. Everyone else just swipes or scrolls their way down ever-darker rabbit holes.

The man who calls himself Lynch is driving much faster than is safe. In his defense, doing a hundred and thirty in the dark down a back road in West Virginia is less dangerous than what lies behind him in the numbered facility that until a few minutes ago was home to his latest project.

He still remembers his first conversation with the Latinx he recruited to handle the more specialized technical aspects of the work. “I don’t know if we can get it back in the bottle,” they said, shaking their head, before signing the contract. Because honestly, nowhere else would be better. If you moved in the right circles in the Valley (and they did), sooner or later you realized that the jokes about founders selling their souls weren’t really jokes. Sooner or later you understood that what lurked behind everyone’s screen was too hungry to ever be satisfied.

They’re dead now, just like the rest of his team. At least he hopes they are, for their sake. He will be too, if he can’t—

Something rises from the road in front of him, bending in ways that aren’t geometrically possible. It’s too late to brake, so he does the only thing he can. He steps on the gas.

Lynch is laughing and sobbing as he dies.

It is January 2025. The man taking the oath of office looks human, but only if you don’t look closely. His flesh seems…loose, and his much younger wife clearly has to stop herself from flinching each time he touches her. Nila Jayaramath idly wonders what really lies beneath his skin and his skull, then closes the video.

Someone knocks on her door. She stands rather stiffly to open it. She has been expecting some sort of visitor for a while.

The young man is wearing a conservative blue suit and a pin-striped tie. Nila does not invite him in. After a moment of uncomfortable silence, he clears his throat. “Ma’am, my name is Levitt. We spoke on the phone. I was hoping…” He pauses, as if suddenly unsure exactly what he was hoping.

Nila doubts his name is actually Levitt, but sees no point in making an issue of the matter. She sees very little point in anything any longer. “I appreciate you coming to see me, Mister Levitt, but I’m afraid my answer has not changed.”

“But professor.” There is desperation in the young man’s voice. “Did you look at the pictures I sent you? The ones from the riot?”

Nila nods. “I did. I’m impressed that you were able to keep them out of the news.” The public saw a man wearing a stupid horned hat surrounded by other human beings. His pictures showed something else.

The young man waves her praise away, as if subverting the world’s media was unworthy of comment. He wastes another few minutes trying to convince Nila to change her mind about helping. Their projections predict a singularity in at most a handful of years. Neither science nor faith have words for what will happen then. When that has no effect, he invokes national security as if it was the opening phrase in an exorcism. Nila is still unmoved, so he gives up and leaves.

Nila goes to stand by her window. Below, students are walking across the quad as they have for a hundred and fifty years. None of them are talking to each other. Instead, they all seem to be looking at the screens in their hands, and at the darkness beneath the glass. She watches for a moment, then lets the curtain fall.

Frml: Next Steps

2026-10-15

Previous posts in this series introduced Frml, explained how it checks simplar scalar code and handles contracts between functions, and then discussed how it deals with loops, checks arrays, and checks built-in functions. The final language level, complete, doesn’t add any new features. Instead, it serves as a placeholder in case we want to extend the language further. I’m not going to do this myself, but if you are a student (or a professor looking for projects for students) please get in touch.

Algebraic Data Types and Pattern Matching

Add enum sum types and a match expression to teach structural induction over data and Z3’s datatype theory. A sample program would be:

enum Option<T> { None, Some(T) }

fn get_or(a: Option<Int>, d: Int) -> Int
  ensures result == d or result != d
{
  match a {
    None => d,
    Some(v) => v,
  }
}

Frml currently proves things about numbers and arrays but never about the shape of a value. Lists, options, and trees are where induction over data structures becomes real, and the classic proofs (list length, append associativity, map distributes over append) are exactly what separates “checked the arithmetic” from “verified a data structure”. Z3 has a native datatype sort, so this is more a type-checker and visitor addition than new solver machinery. It would sit as a datatype level above contracts, and recursive types would reuse the existing decreases machinery.

Explicit Frame Clauses (modifies / reads)

Frml already infers written_params, but the language gives the programmer no way to state what a call may touch. An explicit, checked clause makes that contract visible:

fn increment_first(a: Array<Int>)
  modifies a
  ensures a[0] == old(a[0]) + 1

This would teach the frame problem, modular verification, and information hiding. The pedagogical payoff is the frame problem: proving that a call does not change everything else. A student who writes modifies a and then has the verifier flag an unlisted write learns why modularity fails without framing, and why “what may this function change?” is a first-class specification question rather than an implementation detail. It also becomes the bridge to the hardest topics: aliasing and separation logic, which the current “arrays are references but aliasing is forbidden” design deliberately sidesteps.

Machine Integers (Bit Vectors) vs. Mathematical Integers

Frml’s Int is an unbounded mathematical integer, so x + 1 > x is always true. If we add a bounded type or a --int-model bitvector switch, that theorem quietly breaks:

fn add_one(x: Int32) -> Bool
  ensures x + 1 > x   // false when x == Int32.max

This is arguably the most important caveat in applied verification: the model is not the machine. It teaches why SMT solvers are split into theories, why overflow is a real bug class, and why a verified program can still be wrong if the verification model diverges from the runtime. It also gives a natural way to discuss the tradeoff, since bit-vector reasoning is harder for the solver than integer arithmetic.

Ghost Code and Lemmas

The final idea is to extend the language so that programmers can introduce names and functions that exist only in specifications:

ghost var count: Int = 0;

lemma append_len(a: List<Int>, b: List<Int>)
  ensures length(a ++ b) == length(a) + length(b);

These ghost variables are the classic trick for proving properties that the running program has no reason to record, and lemmas are how a verified fact gets packaged and reused across functions instead of being re-derived inline. Together they teach that a proof is a structure of its own that can be layered on top of, but kept separate from, the executable program, and they unlock the “mathematical library” style of verification that makes large programs tractable. This composes naturally with the first feature idea, since inductive lemmas over data types are the standard way to prove list properties.

Built-in Functions in Frml

2026-10-14

Previous posts in this series introduced Frml, then explained how it checks simplar scalar code, handles contracts between functions, deals with loops, and checks arrays. This post shows how Frml handles built-in functions like push and pop. Here’s an example program to motivate the discussion:

fn main() -> Int
{
  let a: Array<Int> = [1];
  push(a, 2);
  assert length(a) == 2;
  return 0;
}

Built-in Functions

Frml’s built-in functions live in frml/builtins.py:

  • read(path: String) -> String returns the contents of a file
  • write(path: String, text: String) writes text to a file
  • print(text: String) writes text to standard output
  • split(text: String, sep: String) -> Array<String> splits text on a separator
  • args() -> Array<String> returns the command-line arguments
  • push(array, value) appends a value to an array
  • pop(array) -> value removes and returns the last element

TypeChecker.check_call first looks the name up in BUILTINS. For a non-polymorphic built-in, it checks the argument count and each argument type against the recorded signature, then checks that a value-returning built-in is not used as a statement. The same path is reached whether the built-in appears as a statement or as an expression.

push and pop are marked poly=True because their signatures depend on the element type of the array argument: _check_poly_builtin resolves them. push(a, v) requires a to be an array variable and v to have a’s element type; since it is a procedure, it returns no value. pop(a) requires a to be an array variable, returns a’s element type, and cannot be used as a statement. Both are also rejected inside specifications, because a specification must describe a function without mutating arrays.

Verifying Built-Ins

The prover models most built-ins as uninterpreted values: read becomes a fresh string, split and args become fresh arrays of strings, and print and write evaluate their arguments and then have no further effect. The verifier can therefore prove nothing about a built-in’s contents, which is sound because file contents and command-line arguments are external to the program.

push and pop are the only built-ins that mutate a program array, so the prover treats them specially. The prover models push(a, v) as appending v at the current length:

a = Store(a, length(a), v)
length(a) = length(a) + 1

There is no bounds obligation: appending is always legal. The array variable is rebound to the new ArrayVal.

The prover models pop(a) as removing the last element:

result = a[length(a) - 1]
length(a) = length(a) - 1

Unlike push, pop emits an obligation that the array is non-empty:

hypotheses:  current path
goal:        0 < length(a)

Popping an empty array is therefore a failed verification condition rather than a runtime error.

Resizing in Loops

A loop that calls push or pop changes an array’s length, so loop havoc must forget both the contents and the length. A scalar assignment havocs the scalar’s value, a fixed-size array assignment havocs the array’s contents but keeps its length, and an array changed by push or pop havocs the contents and the length. This is the same induction soundness argument as in the loop and array tutorials: the invariant is the only information that survives the havoc.

Resizing Across Function Calls

The builtin prover also tracks which array parameters a callee resizes. written_params contains the array parameters the callee writes, and resized_params is the subset of written_params that the callee resizes with push or pop. At a call site, a written-but-not-resized array parameter gets a fresh post-array with the same length, while a resized array parameter gets a fresh post-array with a fresh, unknown length, and the prover assumes that length is non-negative.

So How Do I Feel About This?

The short answer is “unsatisfied”. I didn’t want to handle push and pop as special cases, and I hoped to build some sort of effects system for I/O, but this project is already larger than I originally intended it to be. If some students want to take this on, please reach out.

Arrays in Frml

2026-10-13

Previous posts in this series introduced Frml, then explained how it checks simplar scalar code, handles contracts between functions, and deals with loops. This post shows how Frml handles arrays.

What We’re Doing

A minimal array program is:

fn main() -> Int
{
  let a: Array<Int> = [2, 3, 5];
  assert a[2] == 5;
  return length(a);
}

The array type checker subclasses the loop type checker and adds visitors for the array syntax. Everything scalar and every while loop is checked exactly as before; the new visitors only handle array nodes.

Array Access

visit_ExprArrayAccess checks two things: the expression being indexed must have an array type, and the index must have type Int. It then returns the array’s element type.

def visit_ExprArrayAccess(self, expr, expected, allow_old, result_type):
    arr = expr.array.accept(self, None, allow_old, result_type)
    FrmlTypeError.fail(
        not arr.is_array(),
        f"array access expects an array but found {arr}",
        expr.pos,
    )
    idx = expr.index.accept(self, None, allow_old, result_type)
    FrmlTypeError.fail(
        idx != INT,
        "array index must have type Int",
        expr.pos,
    )
    return arr.elem

Array Literals

visit_ExprArrayLiteral requires every element to be a scalar (Int, Bool, or String) and all elements to have the same type. It returns Array<T> for that element type. An empty literal [] has no elements from which to infer T, so it is only accepted when the expected type is already known from the surrounding let declaration, parameter type, or return type.

Array Assignment

visit_StmtArrayAssign checks a[i] = v, requiring a to have an array type, i to have type Int, and v to have the array’s element type.

Array declarations

Scalar let declarations are inherited unchanged. For an array declaration, the checker requires the initializer to be either an array literal or a call to a function that returns an array. Array-to-array assignment such as let b: Array<Int> = a; is rejected, because Frml treats arrays as references and allowing that form would create two names for one array; the language avoids that aliasing. (If you want to extend Frml to handle this, please give me a shout.)

Array Returns

A function may return an array, but the checker rejects returning an array parameter directly (again, to avoid aliasing issues). Returning a literal, a function-local array, or a call that returns an array is fine.

def visit_StmtReturn(self, stmt, ret):
    FrmlTypeError.fail(
        (
            ret is not None
            and ret.is_array()
            and stmt.expr.variable_name() in self.current_params
        ),
        "cannot return an array parameter (arrays are references)",
        stmt.pos,
    )
    super().visit_StmtReturn(stmt, ret)

length(...)

visit_ExprLength requires its argument to be an array and returns Int.

The Prover’s Array Model

Arrays need more machinery than integers.

Representation

An array is a Z3 Array(Int, elem) plus a length. The array maps integer indexes to element values, and the length is a separate symbolic integer. The prover stores an array as an ArrayVal, which pairs the Z3 array term with its length. State.arrays maps array variable names to ArrayVal values, and State.old_arrays is the snapshot of those values at function entry.

Array Literals

An array literal like [3, 4] becomes a chain of Store operations:

Store(Store(fresh_array, 0, 3), 1, 4)

Its length is the literal 2. An empty literal produces a fresh array over the expected element sort with length 0.

Access and Assignment

a[i] becomes z3.Select(arr.term, i), and it also emits a bounds obligation 0 <= i and i < length(a). a[i] = v becomes z3.Store(arr.term, i, v), and it also emits a bounds obligation for i.

Bounds Obligations

Every array read and write produces:

hypotheses:  current path
goal:        0 <= i and i < length(a)

For example, from increment_first:

goal:  And(0 >= 0, 0 < a_len!2)

Array Equality

Frml a == b on two arrays is not a single Z3 equality: the lengths must be equal, and every element in range must be equal. The prover builds:

length(a) == length(b)
and
forall i: 0 <= i and i < length(a) => a[i] == b[i]

old(...) with Arrays

old(e) means “the value of e at the moment the function was entered”. The prover snapshots parameters at entry into old_vars and old_arrays.

fn increment_first(a: Array<Int>)
  requires length(a) > 0
  ensures a[0] == old(a[0]) + 1
{
  a[0] = a[0] + 1;
}

At entry, a is a fresh array symbol a!1 with some length, and old_arrays["a"] keeps that original a!1. After the assignment, state.arrays["a"] is the updated array, so a[0] in the postcondition reads the updated array while old(a[0]) reads the original a!1. The emitted postcondition goal looks like:

Store(a!1, 0, a!1[0] + 1)[0] == a!1[0] + 1

Both sides simplify to the same value, so Z3 proves it.

Loops and Arrays

The array level inherits loop verification from the loop level. The only difference is that havoc must also account for arrays. (As a reminder, this is how the prover forgets what a loop did to a variable.) An assigned scalar becomes a fresh symbol of the same sort, and an assigned array becomes a fresh array over the same index and element sorts, keeping its length. At the array language level, lengths never change inside a loop because there is no push or pop (yet).

For a loop body that writes a[i], the prover havocs the contents of a while preserving length(a). This keeps the induction sound: the inductive step does not silently assume which elements were already updated.

while i < length(a)
  invariant forall j: Int :: 0 <= j and j < i => a[j] >= 0
{
  a[i] = a[i] + 1;
  i = i + 1;
}

After havoc, a is a fresh array with the same length, and the invariant is the only bridge from before the loop to after it.

Function Calls with Arrays

When one function calls another, the prover uses the callee’s contract.

Array Arguments

A callee may mutate an array argument. written_params tracks which array parameters a function writes. A written array gets a fresh post-array, while a read-only array keeps the caller’s array unchanged. After the call, the caller’s array argument is updated to the callee’s post-array:

fn increment_first(a: Array<Int>)
  requires length(a) > 0
  ensures a[0] == old(a[0]) + 1
{
  a[0] = a[0] + 1;
}

fn main() -> Int
{
  let xs: Array<Int> = [5];
  increment_first(xs);
  if xs[0] == 6 { return 0; } else { return 1; }
}

increment_first writes a[0], so a is in written_params. At the call increment_first(xs), the precondition length(a) > 0 becomes length(xs) > 0, which Z3 proves from xs = [5] (length 1). The prover creates a fresh post-array a_post!N with the same length, assumes the postcondition a_post!N[0] == xs[0] + 1, and rebinds xs to a_post!N. The later check xs[0] == 6 uses that assumed relationship, so it holds.

Array Returns

A function may return an array, but as noted earlier, it may only return a fresh one, i.e., an array literal, a call to another array-returning function, or a function-local array variable.

At a call site, the returned array is a fresh ArrayVal with an unknown length >= 0, exactly like any other result. The callee’s ensures are assumed, and the well-formedness obligations inside them like array bounds and non-zero divisors are suppressed, because they are part of what is being assumed rather than something the caller proves. For example:

fn first_primes() -> Array<Int>
  ensures length(result) == 4
  ensures result[0] == 2
  ensures result[3] == 7
{
  return [2, 3, 5, 7];
}

fn main() -> Int
{
  let primes: Array<Int> = first_primes();
  assert primes[3] == 7;
  return 0;
}

At the call, primes becomes a fresh array whose length is constrained by ensures length(result) == 4. The later primes[3] is therefore in bounds, and its value is 7 by the second ensures.

Quantifiers Over Arrays

Consider evaluating the body 0 <= i and i < length(a) => a[i] >= 0 inside forall i: Int :: .... a[i] normally emits a bounds obligation 0 <= i and i < length(a) because an out-of-bounds array read is a proof obligation at that program point. Inside a quantifier there is no single program point: i is a bound variable that ranges over all integers, including ones for which 0 <= i and i < length(a) is false. Emitting the bounds obligation there would ask Z3 to prove 0 <= i and i < length(a) for an unconstrained i, which is false, so the verifier would wrongly reject the formula.

Instead, the bounds are part of the quantified formula’s own hypothesis, since the implication only requires a[i] to be well-defined when its left side is true. The prover therefore increments _quant_depth while it evaluates a quantified body, and array accesses and divisions inside that body skip their bounds and division obligations while _quant_depth > 0. The quantified body is still built as a Z3 term, and Z3 reasons about the whole ForAll/Exists formula directly.

Loops in Frml

2026-10-12

The first three posts in this series introduced Frml, then explained how it checks simplar scalar code and contracts between functions. This post shows how Frml handles while loops with invariants and termination measures.

What It Does

Here’s a minimal program with a loop:

fn main() -> Int
{
  let i: Int = 0;
  while i < 3
    invariant 0 <= i
    invariant i <= 3
    decreases 3 - i
  {
    i = i + 1;
  }
  return i;
}

The loop type checker adds one visitor to the existing code called visit_StmtWhile, and the loop prover adds the same visitor plus the logic that turns a loop into obligations. visit_StmtWhile checks four things: the loop condition must have type Bool, every invariant must have type Bool, the decreases expression when present must have type Int, and the loop body is type-checked in a fresh scope.

def visit_StmtWhile(self, stmt, ret):
    self.check_expr(stmt.cond, expected=BOOL, allow_old=False, result_type=None)
    self.in_spec = True
    try:
        for inv in stmt.invariants:
            self.check_expr(inv, expected=BOOL, allow_old=False, result_type=None)
        if stmt.decreases is not None:
            self.check_expr(
                stmt.decreases, expected=INT, allow_old=False, result_type=None
            )
    finally:
        self.in_spec = False
    self._push_scope()
    for s in stmt.body:
        self.check_stmt(s, ret)
    self._pop_scope()

The in_spec flag is set while checking the loop’s specifications, just as the contracts checker does for requires, ensures, and decreases clauses. It has no effect on scalar loops, but the loop checker keeps the pattern so that subsequent levels will inherit the same behavior. The body gets its own scope, so variables declared inside the loop do not leak out of it.

Why Loops Are Hard

A loop runs an unknown number of times, and the prover cannot execute the loop symbolically “until it ends”. It therefore reasons about the loop inductively using invariants. A loop invariant is a fact that is true before the first iteration and after every iteration; if both hold, the invariant is also true immediately after the loop exits. The prover generates one obligation for each of those two moments, plus a post-loop exit state for whatever code follows the loop.

The invariant tells the prover what is true at those moments, but it does not say what value each variable has. That is a real problem: a loop that assigns to a variable may run any number of times, so there is no single value the prover can hand to the rest of the proof. The prover’s answer is to deliberately forget those values as explained in the next section.

Causing Havoc

To havoc a variable is to replace its value with a fresh, unknown symbol, deliberately forgetting everything the prover previously knew about it. This is how the prover stays sound when a value is genuinely unknown: rather than pretending to know the final value of an assigned variable, it admits that it does not.

This is necessary and sound because a loop may run any number of times, so we cannot know the exact final value of an assigned variable; we only know what the invariant tells us about it. The invariant is the only bridge from before the loop to after it.

Before checking the inductive step, the prover scans the loop body and applies havoc to every scalar variable the body assigns. A ScalarWriterCollector walks the body and records every name that appears on the left of an assignment, including assignments inside nested if and while statements. It deliberately ignores let declarations inside the loop body, because those names are local to a single iteration and do not exist before the loop. Each recorded name that already exists in the state is replaced with a fresh symbol of the same Z3 sort:

def _fresh_from_term(self, term, name):
    if z3.is_bool(term):
        return z3.Bool(self._fresh(name))
    if z3.is_string(term):
        return z3.String(self._fresh(name))
    return z3.Int(self._fresh(name))

An Example

Let’s take a look at an example:

fn count(n: Int) -> Int
  requires n >= 0
  ensures result == n
{
  let i: Int = 0;

  while i < n
    invariant 0 <= i
    invariant i <= n
    decreases n - i
  {
    i = i + 1;
  }

  return i;
}

Verifying count emits seven obligations: two initialization, two preservation, two termination, and one final postcondition.

Initialization Obligations

Every invariant must hold before the first iteration. With i = 0, 0 <= i becomes 0 <= 0 and i <= n becomes 0 <= n, proved from requires n >= 0. The prover evaluates each invariant in the state that reaches the while and emits:

hypotheses:  current path
goal:        invariant

Preservation Obligations

This is the inductive step, which checks an arbitrary loop iteration, not just the first one.

  1. Havoc every scalar variable the loop body assigns: i becomes a fresh, unknown symbol such as i!2, which forgets whatever value i had on entry so the check is about an arbitrary iteration.
  2. Assume the invariants and the loop condition, 0 <= i, i <= n, and i < n.
  3. Run the loop body once symbolically, so i = i + 1 becomes i + 1.
  4. Prove each invariant again for the after-body value.

For count, the two preservation obligations are:

(0 <= i and i <= n and i < n) => 0 <= i + 1
(0 <= i and i <= n and i < n) => i + 1 <= n

The second one uses i < n to prove i + 1 <= n.

Exit State

After the loop, the prover must continue with a state that satisfies the invariant and the negated loop condition. It havocs the modified variables again, assumes the invariants, and assumes not condition. For count, after the loop, i becomes a fresh symbol such as i!3, and the path gains 0 <= i, i <= n, and not (i < n), so i >= n. Together these force i == n, and the following return i proves ensures result == n. The body’s final value is deliberately not connected to the exit value; the invariant is the only information that survives.

Termination with decreases

A decreases clause proves that a loop terminates. It produces two obligations.

Before the Loop

Before the loop, the measure must be non-negative:

decreases >= 0

After the Body

After the body, the measure must strictly decrease:

new_decreases < old_decreases

For count, the measure is n - i. On entry, n - 0 >= 0, proved from requires n >= 0. After the body, with the havoced i and the invariant and condition facts assumed, n - (i + 1) < n - i simplifies to -1 < 0. The strict-decrease check uses the same havoced i as the preservation check.

A loop without decreases is still accepted; the verifier then proves partial correctness only. It cannot prove the loop terminates.

A Broken Loop Invariant

A loop invariant must hold before the first iteration and survive every execution of the loop body. The example below survives the first iteration but not the second.

fn bad() -> Bool
{
  let i: Int = 0;
  while i < 3
    invariant i <= 2
  {
    i = i + 1;
  }
  return true;
}

The initialization obligation is 0 <= 2, which holds. The preservation obligation is built exactly like the preservation step in count: havoc i into an arbitrary fresh symbol, assume the invariant i <= 2 and the loop condition i < 3, run the body i = i + 1 once to get the after-body value i + 1, and prove i + 1 <= 2. That gives the obligation:

(i <= 2 and i < 3) => i + 1 <= 2

Z3 finds a counterexample with i = 2. Since 2 <= 2 is true, the invariant holds before the iteration, and since 2 < 3 is true, the loop condition says to run another iteration, but 2 + 1 <= 2 is false, so the invariant is broken after the iteration. The prover does not iterate the loop from 0 to 3; the preservation check symbolically executes the body once on an arbitrary i, and the counterexample i = 2 picks the arbitrary iteration where that one step breaks the invariant. The correct upper bound is i <= 3, because i reaches 3 before the loop exits.

Contracts in Frml

2026-10-10

The first post in this series introduced Frml, a little procedural language I created so that I could learn how program verification works. The second explained how Frml checks simple scalar code; this one explains how it checks programs with multiple functions. As a reminder, the scalar prover owns the engine and leaves two hooks for higher levels:

def verify_function(self, fn):
    state = State()
    for p in fn.params:
        state.vars[p.name] = self._fresh_scalar(p.type, p.name)
    self._assume_spec(fn, state)
    end_states = self.exec_stmt_seq(fn.body, state)
    if fn.return_type is not None:
        if end_states:
            raise FrmlVerificationError(...)
    else:
        for s in end_states:
            self._check_ensures(fn, s, None)

_assume_spec and _check_ensures do nothing at the scalar level; the contracts prover overrides them:

class Prover(ScalarProver):
    def _assume_spec(self, fn, state):
        for req in fn.requires:
            state.path.append(self.eval_expr(req, state))
        if fn.decreases is not None:
            d = self.eval_expr(fn.decreases, state)
            self.entry_decreases = d
            self._emit(
                "decreases",
                f"decreases {fn.decreases.render()} >= 0",
                state.path,
                d >= 0,
                fn.decreases.pos,
            )

    def _check_ensures(self, fn, state, result):
        for ens in fn.ensures:
            self.check_postcondition(ens, state, result)

requires and ensures

Contracts are the source of most hypotheses and goals. At function entry, every requires clause is evaluated, the result is appended to the path, and later obligations may assume it. At every return, every ensures clause is evaluated, the result placeholder is replaced by the returned expression, and the result becomes a goal checked against the current path. Multiple requires clauses are and’ed, one fact per clause in the path, and multiple ensures clauses produce one obligation per clause. For example:

fn max(a: Int, b: Int) -> Int
  ensures result >= a
  ensures result >= b
  ensures result == a or result == b
{
  if a >= b {
    return a;
  } else {
    return b;
  }
}

The if produces two end states, and there are three ensures clauses, so there are six postcondition obligations, three per branch. The if branch goals are a >= a, a >= b, and a == a or a == b; the else branch goals are b >= a, b >= b, and b == a or b == b. Each is proved with the appropriate branch condition in the path.

check_postcondition turns an ensures clause into one obligation:

def check_postcondition(self, ens, state, result):
    goal = self.eval_expr(ens, state, result_term=result)
    self._emit("postcondition", ens.render(), state.path, goal, ens.pos)

result_term supplies the term that result denotes inside the clause.

A Complete Trace

Let’s trace a tiny function through the whole machine:

fn double(x: Int) -> Int
  ensures result == x + x
{
  return x + x;
}

fn main() -> Int
{
  return double(4);
}
  1. verify_program builds the Prover and calls verify().
  2. verify() sets current_fn = double and calls verify_function(double).
  3. verify_function builds the entry state:
    • state.vars["x"] = Int("x!1"): a fresh integer symbol.
    • state.old_vars["x"] = Int("x!1"): the old(...) snapshot.
    • No requires, no decreases, so _assume_spec leaves the path [].
  4. exec_stmt_seq([return x + x], state) starts with states = [state].
  5. The single statement is return x + x, so exec_stmt calls visit_StmtReturn.
  6. eval_rhs(x + x, state) falls through to eval_expr(x + x, state):
    • visit_ExprBinary sees the operator +.
    • The left x becomes Int("x!1"); the right x becomes Int("x!1").
    • It returns the Z3 term x!1 + x!1.
  7. Back in visit_StmtReturn, value = x!1 + x!1.
  8. _check_ensures runs for the single ensures result == x + x:
    • It calls check_postcondition, which evaluates result == x + x with result_term = x!1 + x!1.
    • result looks up result_term, giving x!1 + x!1.
    • Each x looks up state.vars["x"], giving x!1.
    • The goal is the Z3 term (x!1 + x!1) == (x!1 + x!1).
    • One obligation is emitted with the empty path [].
  9. visit_StmtReturn returns [], so states becomes [] and the loop breaks.
  10. end_states == []; double has a return type and no fall-through path, so no error is raised.
  11. verify() records ProverResult("double", [the one obligation]).
  12. verify_program hands the obligation to check_obligations, which asks Z3 to refute not (True => (x!1 + x!1) == (x!1 + x!1)). No value of x!1 makes it true, so Z3 answers unsat and the obligation is VERIFIED.

old(...)

old(e) means “the value of e at the moment the function was entered”. The prover snapshots parameters at entry into old_vars; inside an ensures clause, old(x) reads that snapshot rather than the current value. For example:

fn bump(x: Int) -> Int
  ensures result == old(x) + 1
{
  x = x + 1;
  return x;
}

At entry, x is a fresh integer symbol x!1, and old_vars["x"] keeps that original x!1. After the assignment, state.vars["x"] is the term x!1 + 1, so x in the postcondition reads the updated value while old(x) reads the original x!1. The emitted postcondition goal looks like:

x!1 + 1 == x!1 + 1

Both sides are the same term, so Z3 proves it.

Function Calls

When one function calls another, the prover uses the callee’s contract. For a statement call f(args), the prover proves the callee’s requires clauses under the caller’s current path, assumes the callee’s ensures clauses, and adds those assumptions to the caller’s path. The caller never sees the callee’s body; it only sees the callee’s contract. For example:

fn inc(x: Int) -> Int
  requires x >= 0
  ensures result == x + 1
{ return x + 1; }

fn main() -> Int
{
  return inc(41);
}

For the call inc(41), the prover emits a precondition obligation 41 >= 0, creates a fresh result inc_result!N, and assumes inc_result!N == 41 + 1. main returns that result, and the postcondition is proved.

Recursion

Recursive functions need a decreases clause. At function entry, _assume_spec emits decreases >= 0. For factorial, that is n >= 0, proved from requires n >= 0. At a recursive call, the callee’s requires clauses are proved at the call site. For factorial(n - 1), the prover proves n - 1 >= 0.

model_call emits decreases_at_call < decreases_at_entry for a self-call. This applies to statement calls; for scalar self-calls inside expressions, such as the recursive call in factorial below, the current implementation proves the callee’s precondition but does not emit the strict-decrease obligation.

fn factorial(n: Int) -> Int
  requires n >= 0
  ensures result >= 1
  decreases n
{
  if n == 0 {
    return 1;
  } else {
    return n * factorial(n - 1);
  }
}

The prover emits four obligations: n >= 0 for the entry decreases n >= 0, 1 >= 1 for the base-case postcondition, n - 1 >= 0 for the recursive call’s precondition, and n * factorial_result >= 1 for the recursive postcondition.

Quantifiers

Frml supports forall and exists in specifications:

forall i: Int :: 0 <= i and i < 3 => i >= 0

The prover translates these to Z3 quantifiers: forall becomes z3.ForAll([var], body) and exists becomes z3.Exists([var], body). The quantified variable becomes a fresh symbol, and the body is evaluated with that variable added to the state. In the example above, the variable i becomes a fresh symbol added to the state, the body becomes the Z3 term Implies(And(0 <= i, i < 3), i >= 0), and the whole quantifier becomes z3.ForAll([i], body).

Similarly, if exists was used, the variable i would again become a fresh symbol, the body would become And(0 <= i, i < 3, i == 2), and the whole quantifier would become z3.Exists([i], body). Both forms can appear in an ensures clause:

fn f() -> Bool
  ensures result == (forall i: Int :: 0 <= i and i < 3 => i >= 0)
{ return true; }

fn g() -> Bool
  ensures result == (exists i: Int :: 0 <= i and i < 3 and i == 2)
{ return true; }
